Salud × Cedars-Sinai

From Risk Assessment to Trusted Next Steps

Salud is an AI-supported cancer screening navigation and community outreach experience designed with Cedars-Sinai for Latinx communities in Antelope Valley. Situated in a grocery-store setting, the service uses a guided kiosk survey, personalized takeaways, and on-site promotoras to turn screening questions into approachable next steps.

Context

Access was not the only barrier. Trust mattered too.

Our project focused on Latinx adults in Antelope Valley, one of the communities already reached through Cedars-Sinai’s outreach work. Cost concerns, distrust of healthcare systems, and the belief that screening is unnecessary without symptoms can make colorectal cancer screening feel distant. Through secondary research and conversations with promotoras, Cedars-Sinai’s community health workers, we learned how they bridge this gap through shared language, cultural understanding, and trusted relationships.

Technology could extend access to information, but it needed to support the human relationships that make people willing to engage.

Pivot

Risk prediction created the wrong relationship.

Our early concepts used grocery purchases or self-reported dietary habits to estimate colorectal cancer risk. Voluntary input gave users more control over what they shared, but it did not resolve the larger issue. In a public setting, personalized risk scoring could expose sensitive information, create a sense of judgment, and position AI as a medical authority. The problem was not only how the data was collected. It was the assumption that this system should draw cancer-risk conclusions about a person at all.

We deliberately limited AI’s role: it would support informed next steps, not evaluate personal cancer risk.
Final Service

Bringing screening support into everyday life

We designed Salud as a promotora-supported service concept for a Ralphs grocery store, a familiar place residents may already visit each week. Rather than requiring people to enter the healthcare system to seek information, the service created a low-pressure starting point within a daily routine. The kiosk extended the reach of Cedars-Sinai’s outreach work, while an on-site promotora remained available for questions and personal guidance.

1. Discover
A “Free Tote Bag” prompt and nearby signage offer a low-pressure reason to approach the kiosk, with an on-site promotora available to introduce the experience.
2. Share
Users complete a 10-question survey about their screening awareness, concerns, and preferences.
3. Learn
Each response is followed by short, relevant feedback rather than a score or risk result.
4. Take away
After completing the survey, users receive a tote bag and a personalized summary with a suggested next step, screening information, and a Cedars-Sinai resource.
5. Connect
The experience ends with an invitation to speak with the on-site promotora and an optional choice to receive future follow-up from Cedars-Sinai.

Design Decisions

Designing for support, not assessment

Respond without scoring
Each answer receives relevant information without assigning a score or risk level, keeping the experience responsive without judging the user.
Limit personalization to guidance
Survey responses shape the information and next steps users receive, but the system does not diagnose or predict cancer risk.
Lead toward human support
The closing screen and summary connect users with an on-site promotora for questions and personal guidance.
Reflection

This project challenged my assumption that a more capable AI would automatically create a stronger product. In sensitive contexts, more capability can create distance rather than value when a system takes on more authority than people can reasonably trust it to hold. Designing responsible AI is not only about expanding what it can do. It is also about deciding what it should not do.
