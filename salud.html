Salud

From Risk Assessment to Trusted Next Steps

overview

Salud is a colorectal cancer (CRC) screening service concept designed with Cedars-Sinai for Latinx adults aged 45–80 in Antelope Valley. It explores how an AI-supported kiosk can extend Cedars-Sinai’s outreach to underserved communities, with community health workers remaining central to personal guidance.

my contribution

I owned the kiosk’s end-to-end interaction and visual design, from every screen and per-question feedback to privacy interactions, AI boundaries, and the physical enclosure. The research and the ten survey questions were developed collaboratively by the team.

Context

Access was not the only barrier. Trust mattered too.

To understand what keeps people from screening, we combined secondary research with conversations with Cedars-Sinai’s community health workers (CHWs), who help residents access health information and care. Cost, healthcare distrust, and the belief that screening is unnecessary without symptoms can limit screening participation.

CHWs are known as promotoras in Spanish-speaking communities.

Research found lower CRC screening participation among immigrant adults than among U.S.-born adults: 52% compared with 73% (Rosowicz & Hewitt, 2025).

In one culturally tailored, community-based intervention, CRC screening completion increased from 18% to 55% (Aragones et al., 2010).

CHWs bridge this gap through shared language, cultural understanding, and trusted relationships. Meaningful engagement depended on human trust, not information alone, so our challenge became how AI could help carry some of that trust into everyday life.

Pivot

Risk prediction created the wrong relationship.

Our early concepts used grocery purchases or self-reported dietary habits to estimate CRC risk.

Early storyboard, abandoned concept. The kiosk scans groceries and returns a risk percentage.

Voluntary input gave users more control over what they shared. But in a public setting, personalized risk scoring could expose sensitive information, create a sense of judgment, and position AI as a medical authority. The problem was not only how the data was collected. It was the assumption that this system should draw cancer-risk conclusions about a person at all.

We deliberately limited AI’s role: it would support informed next steps, not evaluate personal cancer risk.

Final Service

Bringing screening support into everyday life

We looked for a familiar, nonclinical touchpoint within Cedars-Sinai’s existing Antelope Valley outreach area. We proposed a Ralphs grocery store because residents might already visit it regularly, allowing screening information and CHW support to become part of an everyday routine. Its public setting also shaped our boundaries: no personal risk scoring, no exposed health information, and no data retention without opt-in.

What happens
A “Free Tote Bag” prompt and nearby signage offer a low-pressure reason to approach the kiosk, with an on-site CHW available to introduce the experience.
What happens
Users complete a 10-question survey about their screening awareness, concerns, and preferences.
What happens
Each response is followed by short, relevant feedback rather than a score or risk result.
Design decision
Each answer receives relevant information without assigning a score or risk level, keeping the experience responsive without judging the user.
What happens
After completing the survey, users receive a tote bag and a tailored summary with a suggested next step, screening information, and a Cedars-Sinai resource.
Design decision
Survey responses shape the information and next steps users receive, but the system does not diagnose or predict cancer risk. The AI’s proposed role is limited to organizing a session’s answers into a tailored summary and suggested next step; we did not select, build, or validate a model.
What happens
The experience ends with an invitation to speak with the on-site CHW and an optional choice to receive future follow-up from Cedars-Sinai.
Design decision
The closing screen and summary connect users with an on-site CHW for questions and personal guidance.

Outcome and Next Validation

Delivered: an end-to-end clickable kiosk prototype with simulated printing, a physical enclosure, a summary ticket, and a souvenir system. In final stakeholder feedback, Cedars-Sinai responded positively to the CHW integration, grocery-store setting, and printed takeaway. The concept was designed for a Ralphs setting; it was not deployed or tested with residents.

Next, I would evaluate whether the grocery-store environment allows people to participate comfortably and validate the experience directly with residents. I would also prototype the printing workflow, localize the experience in Spanish, and define the AI and data-retention rules with clinical and privacy stakeholders. The name field would move to the opt-in screen, so identity enters the system only when someone requests follow-up.

Reflection

This project challenged my assumption that a more capable AI would automatically create a stronger product. In sensitive contexts, more capability can create distance rather than value when a system takes on more authority than people can reasonably trust it to hold. Designing responsible AI is not only about expanding what it can do. It is also about deciding what it should not do.
