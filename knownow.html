KnowNow

Closing the gap between alert and action

overview

A multilingual, map-based emergency response app that helps people understand how a nearby threat affects them, find a safe destination, and take the next step quickly.

my contribution

KnowNow began as a 14-week team project that ended as a low-fi prototype. I then independently redesigned it, reframing the problem and rebuilding the product strategy, information architecture, interactions, and prototype.

Context

A crisis close to home

The 2025 Los Angeles wildfires did not feel like a distant disaster. They affected the community around me, including classmates and faculty at ArtCenter. Beyond the visible destruction, I became interested in a less visible crisis: critical emergency information was not equally accessible across languages. Some non-English speakers could receive an alert yet still struggle to understand the danger, decide whether it affected them, or find help.

This led us to ask how emergency communication could become more accessible. But as the project developed, we discovered that translation was only one part of the gap between receiving an alert and knowing what to do next.

Research

The people the system never sees

We interviewed multilingual volunteers, an interpreter, emergency responders, and people affected by the fires, and visited a Red Cross shelter. Inside, support appeared to work well. But that view only included people who had already understood the emergency and reached help. Others relied on family or community members to interpret alerts, while some felt uncomfortable asking for translation. The people facing the greatest language barriers could disappear before the formal support system ever saw them.

Field visit — Red Cross emergency shelter, Los Angeles, January 2025

People scan, they don’t read.
Under pressure, long text and detailed explanations get skipped.
Understanding matters more than translation.
A translated alert can still be misread. Maps, icons, and color carry meaning faster than words.
“Am I safe?” comes first.
People need to see where they are relative to the risk before they can decide what to do.
Decisions happen in seconds.
The interface has to support immediate recognition, not exploration.

Reframing the Problem

From universal access to personal action

By the end of the team project, we had expanded the challenge from helping non-English speakers seek support to creating universally accessible emergency communication. However, this framing was broader than the mobile experience we had designed. When I revisited the project independently, I narrowed the problem around a more immediate question: what does someone need to understand and do after receiving an alert?

Team’s final HMW
How might we design and implement universally accessible communication systems to overcome linguistic barriers during emergency response operations?
My reframed HMW
How might we help people understand how an emergency affects them and take the next safe action?

Structure

From parallel features to one sequence

The team concept offered five parallel tools: news, training, alerts, shelters, and a packing list. In the redesign, I rebuilt the system around a smaller set of core objects, each answering one question a person asks during an emergency.

Two ways in: opening the app means exploring, a notification means urgency.
1Alert
Am I in danger?
2Map
Where am I?
3Shelter
Where should I go?
4Route
How do I get there?

Each object exists to move people toward their next step. The system is no longer about what users can do, but about what they should do next.

Refined user flow, from alert to a safe route

Iteration

Testing the first screen

A notification is often the first contact with the app, so I tested the landing screen in two rounds of walkthroughs with an AI-simulated persona. These were not sessions with real users, but they showed where the screen failed to explain itself.

Say where the user stands

A status line such as “You are near the danger zone” or “You are inside the hazard zone” answers the first question before anything else.

Lead with one action

The bottom panel names the next step and offers a single primary button, instead of a generic “View details.”

Recommend one shelter

The nearest open shelter is highlighted rather than shown as one equal option among many.

Final Design

One path from alert to action

I redesigned KnowNow as a map-based experience that connects each alert to the user’s location, current emergency state, available destinations, and next action.

What happens
A lock-screen alert names the hazard, its distance, and the action to take.
Design decision
The alert leads with the action, so someone who only glances at the lock screen still knows what to do.
What happens
Tapping the alert opens the map centered on the user, with the hazard zone, their status, and one primary action: start a route to a shelter.
Design decision
Status comes before detail. The screen answers “Am I safe?” before offering anything else.
What happens
Shelters are listed by distance, with capacity and services such as pets, ADA access, food, and medical care.
Design decision
The nearest open shelter is highlighted, so choosing a destination does not become one more decision under stress.
What happens
The shelter detail confirms availability and address, and Navigate opens the route in the user’s default map app.
Design decision
Navigation is handed to a map app people already know, rather than rebuilding turn-by-turn directions.
What happens
From the profile, users share their live location with emergency contacts and set their language, alert translation, and medical and accessibility needs.
Design decision
Medical and accessibility details are shared with responders only when the user requests help.

Reflection

The redesign transformed KnowNow from a collection of emergency tools into a connected path from alert to action. Because the landing screen was refined through AI-simulated walkthroughs rather than sessions with real users, the next step would be to validate its language, emergency states, and action guidance with multilingual residents and emergency-response professionals.
